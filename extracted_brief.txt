TF-FaultNet in Plain Language
What we built, why it works, and what to say about it
For: the project team
One-line version: we point a microphone at a machine, and a neural network tells us which
of 12 things is wrong with it.
1. The problem
Factories and cars are full of spinning parts — belts, pulleys, gears, bearings. When one
starts wearing out, catching it early saves a lot of money. Catching it late destroys the
machine.
The normal way to catch it is a vibration sensor bolted onto the machine. That works,
but it's expensive, and you have to physically open up and modify every machine you want to
monitor.
Our idea: just listen to it. A microphone costs almost nothing, touches nothing, and can be
moved anywhere. The question is whether sound alone carries enough information.
It does. That's the whole project.
2. What makes it hard
Three things get in the way:
Faults sound completely diﬀerent from each other. A slipping belt makes a continuous
scraping noise that runs through the whole recording. A gear with a missing tooth makes
one sharp click every rotation and is silent in between. A model that's good at spotting one is
usually bad at the other.
Machines hum constantly. Shaft rotation, motor noise, 50/60 Hz from the mains. That hum
is present in every single recording — healthy or broken — so it's pure distraction.
It's easy to fool yourself. If your test data accidentally contains clips from the same
recording session as your training data, your model can score 99% by recognising the room,
not the fault. A lot of published work has this problem.

3. How the system works
Step 1 — Turn sound into a picture
Raw audio is just a wiggly line, 44,100 numbers per second. Hard to learn from.
So we convert each 1-second clip into a spectrogram — a picture where the horizontal axis
is time, the vertical axis is pitch, and brightness is how much energy is there. A belt slip
shows up as a bright horizontal smear. A broken tooth shows up as thin bright vertical
stripes.
Once it's a picture, we can use image-recognition techniques on it.
The one clever bit: most projects make these pictures once, save them as ﬁles, and train
on the ﬁles. We compute them inside the model, on the GPU, every time. That means no
storage cost, and the model can learn all the way back to the raw audio instead of stopping
at a frozen image.
Step 2 — Look at the picture
TF-FaultNet is a fairly standard image classiﬁer (a ResNet) with two additions that exist
speciﬁcally because of the problems in section 2:

Dual pooling is worth understanding properly. If you only take the average, a single
millisecond click gets diluted into nothing — you'd miss every tooth-loss fault. If you only
take the peak, a continuous scrape has no peak to ﬁnd — you'd miss every belt fault. So we
take both and hand the classiﬁer both numbers.
Step 3 — Prove we're not cheating
Four tests, in the order you'd run them:
1. Can it memorise 24 clips? If not, something is broken in the code. It got 100%.
2. Shuﬄe the labels and retrain. Accuracy should collapse to random guessing. It fell to
8.84%, and random for 12 classes is 8.33%. So the model isn't picking up some artifact of
the recording setup.
3. Does validation loss track training loss? Yes, gap of 0.0115. If it had ballooned, we'd
be memorising.
4. Train on early recordings, test on later ones. This is the honest one. 97.16%.

4. The results
Model Accuracy
TF-FaultNet (ours) 99.77% ± 0.21%
WaveformCNN1D 99.67% ± 0.24%
AudioBiGRU 99.30% ± 0.33%
XGBoost 96.46% ± 1.04%
12 classes, 2,148 clips, 179 per class, ﬁve-fold cross-validation.
Now read that table honestly. The gap between us and WaveformCNN1D is 0.1
percentage points — about two clips out of 2,148 — and the error bars overlap completely.
We cannot claim our model is more accurate than that baseline. What the table actually
shows is that three diﬀerent deep models all max out this dataset, and only the simple
24-feature approach falls behind.
That's ﬁne. It just means we sell the architecture on diﬀerent grounds: it's interpretable (you
can look at the spectrogram and the attention weights and see why), it maps onto the
physics of the faults, and it doesn't need oﬄine preprocessing.
5. Which number to quote
Quote 97.16%, not 99.77%.
The 99.77% comes from a random split, where clips from one recording session can land in
both training and test. The 97.16% comes from holding out entire later sessions — genuinely
unheard recordings. That 2.6-point gap is roughly the size you'd expect from session-level
leakage, which is exactly why the second number is the defensible one.
Say it ﬁrst, before anyone asks. It reads as rigour. If a reviewer has to dig it out of you, it
reads as something you were hiding.
6. Questions to be ready for
"Isn't 99.77% suspiciously high?" Yes, and it's because the task is easier than a real
factory: controlled testbench, balanced classes, clean recordings. Say so. Then pivot: the
point isn't the ceiling, it's that a contactless mic reaches accuracy people currently need
bolted-on accelerometers for.
"Why is your model better than the 1D baseline?" On accuracy, it isn't — the diﬀerence
is noise. It's better on interpretability, on physics justiﬁcation, and on not needing cached

"Did you check for data leakage?" Yes — chronological session holdout, 97.16%. Also the

"Where are your ablations?" We don't have them, and this is our real weakness. SE
attention and dual pooling are justiﬁed by argument, not by measurement. Removing each
one and re-running the ﬁve folds is the single most valuable thing left to do.
7. Five things to remember
1. Microphone instead of a bolted-on sensor. That's the pitch.
2. Sound becomes a picture, and the picture is computed inside the model, not saved to
disk.
3. SE attention removes the hum; dual pooling catches both smeared and spiky faults.
4. Lead with 97.16% (unseen sessions), not 99.77%.
5. Our biggest gap is the missing ablations. Own it before someone else points at it.
